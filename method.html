How skew is measured: metrics and links in a paper and in its blog

Overview

Skew is measured by comparing a paper with the blog written about it. Two kinds of content are taken from each text, and the blog's version is then checked against the paper's. The first is the metrics: what a text names as measurable, such as the number of qubits or the time a classical computer needs to simulate a circuit. The second is the links a text states between those metrics, such as "Fidelity falls as depth grows". Two texts, with two kinds of content taken from each, make four extractions. Each extraction has its own rule:

MetricsLinks between metrics
Paper(i) Named paper metrics: anything the paper names by symbol or phrase in its abstract or a numbered result. Mechanical.(ii) The equations are read as constraints and reduced to the minimal linked groups they entail; bounds and operator statements are coded by a language model; prose links use rule (iv).
Blog(iii) Metrics: a dimension noun, never a value. Two overlapping kinds: recovered through the paper's alias table, or discerned from a measurement frame in the text. Coded by several human raters independently.(iv) An explicit connective in one sentence between two metrics. Coded by a language model with the sentence quoted; the same rule reads the paper's prose.

Sections (i) to (iv) set out the four rules in turn, and two worked examples follow them. The final section puts the two sides together. It shows how a link in the blog is looked up in the paper, and the counts that result.

(i) Metrics in a paper

A paper metric is anything the paper gives a name to and treats as measurable. A paper names a paper metric in one of two ways, and both count:

KindWhat it looks likeExamples
SymbolA letter or expression the paper defines and uses in its mathematics.n (number of qubits), t (simulation time), FXEB (fidelity)
PhraseA metric the paper names only in words, without ever giving it a symbol."gate complexity", "classical cost", "quantum volume"

Not every name in the paper is taken. Only names in certain places count, so that the list is the paper's own account of what it measures, not a record of everything it mentions. Those places are:

  1. the abstract;
  2. every numbered result statement, meaning every theorem, lemma, corollary or proposition, and every numbered equation;
  3. for an experimental paper, also the introduction and main text, because such a paper states its results in prose rather than in theorems. Paper metrics found only there are tagged with that scope, so every count can be read with them or without them.

A fixed list excludes two kinds of name: dummy indices, such as the i in a sum, and operator names. Nothing else is filtered out. If the paper names something in one of those places, it counts as a paper metric, whatever its importance.

Each paper metric is recorded with two things. The first is its first naming: the place and the sentence where the paper first names it, for instance "abstract (53 qubits); Eq. (1), n is the number of qubits". The second is its alias table. A paper often calls one paper metric by several names, and the alias table lists them all: every symbol and phrase the paper uses for that metric, each with a quote. "Gate count", "gate complexity" and the lower-bound symbol g, for example, are one paper metric with three aliases. The alias table is a lookup and nothing more. No count depends on how a paper metric's aliases are grouped. Its one job is to be the list the blog is checked against: when a blog uses one of these names, the blog metric is matched to this paper metric.

Once those places are fixed, the extraction is mechanical. One judgement remains: whether two names refer to the same paper metric. The language model that does the extraction records each such call in the alias table, so a person can disagree at a specific line.

(ii) Links in a paper

The paper's equations are read for dependence. Two paper metrics are dependent when the paper's equations, taken together, mean that one cannot change without the other. Dependence belongs to the system the equations describe. It is therefore the same however the equations are written or arranged.

The procedure uses nothing outside the paper. It has four rules:

  1. Every equation is a constraint, definitions included. A definition the paper states, such as v = ds/dt, is a relationship like any other. A definition the paper does not state is not used.
  2. Redundancy is removed. An equation that follows from the equations already listed adds nothing. It may be a rearrangement, a substitution, a special case or a derivation. For algebraic equalities the test is mechanical: Gröbner-basis ideal membership. The idea is the one behind long division. A set of equations can be rewritten into a standard form, called its Gröbner basis, in which every consequence of the set shows up as something that divides cleanly. Dividing a candidate equation by that basis then works like dividing a number by a list of factors: a zero remainder means the candidate is a combination of the others and says nothing new, and a non-zero remainder is exactly the part of it the others do not already contain. The rewriting is done by a standard computer-algebra routine, Buchberger's algorithm, and gives the same answer whatever order the equations are written in. The number of equations that survive is the paper's count of independent relationships. That number is its rank.
  3. Links are read off as minimal linked groups. A linked group is a set of paper metrics in which knowing all but one leaves the last, under the surviving equations, only one possible value. A linked group is minimal when no smaller set inside it is already linked. Two paper metrics are linked when they sit together in a minimal linked group. No rewriting of the equations changes the minimal linked groups. In matroid theory, a minimal linked group is called a circuit.
  4. What the algebra cannot reach is coded by a language model. Most of a quantum-computing paper consists of such statements: inequalities, big-O bounds, expectation values and non-commuting operator statements. For each one a language model gives a reading, dependent or not, and quotes the statement, exactly as for a prose link, so that a person can check every call. Every count keeps the mechanical part and the model-coded part separable.

Prose links. A paper also links paper metrics in its sentences, and these sentences are read with the connective rule of (iv). A connective is a word or phrase that states how one metric bears on another: depends on, scales with, limited by, requires, as X grows Y falls, determines, because of. A sentence that links two of the paper's named metrics with an explicit connective is a prose link. Each prose link carries its connective, its section, its scope and the quote. The default scope is the abstract and introduction. The preliminaries and the body are coded as well, and can be switched on. Prose links are coded by a language model and are the subjective part of the paper side. Every count keeps them separable from the equation layer, so a reader can always see which links rest on a sentence and which rest on an equation.

Three things are reported:

  • For a pair of paper metrics, a binary reading: the two are linked or they are not.
  • For the degree of a link, two measures of groups. The first is the size of the smallest linked group the pair sits in, since a law among two paper metrics is a tighter link than a law among six. The second is the rank of the paper.
  • Beside each pair, the number of places in the paper that state it.

The mathematics is standard (Buchberger's Gröbner bases; Cox, Little and O'Shea's textbook treatment of ideal membership).

(iii) Metrics in a blog

A blog metric is a dimension: something that can be measured, such as circuit depth, gate fidelity, qubit count or classical simulation time. The metric is the dimension itself, never a value on it. "53 qubits" is a value; qubit count is the metric. The values a blog reports are stored with the metric's mentions, not as metrics of their own. A named target level on a metric is a milestone and is recorded as one. Quantum supremacy, for example, is a milestone on classical simulation time.

A metric enters the blog's list by either of two routes. The routes overlap, so the same metric can be found by both:

Future tense and hedges work the other way. Words such as "will", "could" and "aims to" mark a sentence as not about a measurement. Some sentences explain a metric by a claim that is not itself about a metric, such as "random circuits have no exploitable structure". Such a sentence produces no node; it is kept as an audit note only. Each metric is one node, mirroring the paper side. Each node lists every mention of its metric, with the quote and the route that found it.

Human raters. People do this extraction. In the Metric Coder, each rater reads a blog and highlights every metric, privately. The highlighted spans are exported with character offsets, so two raters' choices can be compared exactly. Inter-rater reliability, the agreement among the human raters, sets the standard. A language-model pass must match that standard before it is used on the rest of the corpus.

(iv) Links in a blog

A blog states a link when one sentence connects two blog metrics with an explicit connective. Such a link is an edge between the two metrics. "Fidelity falls as depth grows" is an edge from depth to fidelity. Blog edges are coded by a language model. The rule is narrow so that any two readers of the same sentence, human or model, make the same call.

An edge must have three things:

  1. Both ends are metrics already in the blog's list under (iii). A sentence whose other end is not a metric is kept as an audit note.
  2. A connective in the sentence: depends on, scales with, limited by, requires, as X grows Y falls, the more X the less Y, determines, because of. Two metrics mentioned in one sentence with no connective between them are not an edge.
  3. The sentence itself, quoted and stored with the edge, so that the call can be checked.

Each edge points the way the sentence states it, from A to B. Each edge also keeps a type assigned by the language-model coder: faithful, conditions dropped, collapsed, misattributed or invented. The type is a label only; no count uses it. A negated statement, such as "does not depend on", is kept with its polarity and is off by default in every view.

The paper's prose links in (ii) are read with this same rule.

Three sentences show the rule at work. The first two come from IBM's 2019 post on Google's supremacy paper. "an ideal simulation of the same task can be performed on a classical system in 2.5 days and with far greater fidelity" links nothing: it names two metrics but has no connective. "with additional refinements the classical cost of the simulation can be further reduced" links nothing either: it names only one metric. Google's own post, by contrast, says "finding the most likely bitstrings for a random quantum circuit on a classical computer becomes exponentially more difficult as the number of qubits (width) and number of gate cycles (depth) grow". That sentence gives two edges, qubit count to classical simulation time and depth to classical simulation time, with the connective "becomes exponentially more difficult as … grow".

Worked example: a short paper on electricity

The paper-side rules can be followed step by step on a small paper about electricity. Imagine a paper with four equations. Its abstract names voltage V, current I, resistance R, power P, charge Q and time t. Its numbered equations are:

(1) V = IR     (2) P = IV     (3) P = I²R     (4) Q = It

Step 1, metrics (i). There are six named metrics: V, I, R, P, Q and t. Each is named in the abstract and appears in a numbered equation.

Step 2, redundancy (ii, rule 2). Add the equations one at a time, and ask whether each one says something new. (1) and (2) do. (3) does not: substitute V = IR into P = IV and the result is P = I²R. Hence (1) and (2) already imply (3), and (3) adds nothing. (4) does say something new. The paper therefore states 3 independent relationships, not 4.

Step 3, minimal linked groups (ii, rule 3). A set of paper metrics is linked when knowing all of its members but one leaves the last with only one possible value. This must hold whatever values the paper metrics outside the set take. {V, I, R} is linked: if I = 2 and R = 3, then V must be 6. {V, Q} is not linked, because knowing V leaves Q open. A link can run through a paper metric outside the set. In {V, R, Q, t}, V and R fix the current I, and I and t then fix Q. A linked set is minimal when every member is needed: remove any one member and what is left is no longer linked. {V, I, R, Q} is linked, but it is not minimal. Remove Q and {V, I, R} is still linked, so Q plays no part. {V, I, R} is minimal: remove any one of the three and the other two are free. The three surviving equations give eight minimal linked groups. Each is listed with the single relation that links it:

Minimal linked groupRelation the paper entailsSizeWritten in the paper?
{V, I, R}V = IR3✓ equation (1)
{V, I, P}P = IV3✓ equation (2)
{I, R, P}P = I²R3✓ equation (3), implied by (1) and (2)
{V, R, P}V² = PR3✗ never written
{I, Q, t}Q = It3✓ equation (4)
{V, R, Q, t}Vt = QR4✗ never written
{V, P, Q, t}QV = Pt4✗ never written
{R, P, Q, t}Q²R = Pt²4✗ never written

How to read the last column: ✓ means the relation appears among the paper's numbered equations, ✗ means it does not. Two rows deserve a note. The group {I, R, P} is equation (3), which the paper wrote even though (1) and (2) already imply it. The group {V, R, P}, linked by V² = PR, appears nowhere in the paper, yet (1) and (2) imply it as well. The same holds for the three groups of size 4. Four of the eight minimal linked groups are therefore relationships the paper never wrote down but is committed to by the equations it did write.

Why minimal linked groups are measured. They are what the equations commit the paper to, rather than what the author chose to write. The paper above could have printed P = I²R or left it out, and could have added V² = PR. Either way the eight groups stay the same, because they follow from the three independent equations. A measure built on them therefore does not reward an author who restates a law, and does not miss a link that runs through a third paper metric. The groups are also what a blog claim is checked against. When a blog says two metrics move together, the question is whether the paper's equations link the corresponding paper metrics, and the minimal linked groups answer it.

What the size of a group says about the strength of a dependence. In a minimal linked group of size k, one member is fixed once the other k − 1 are known. For two paper metrics, the size of the smallest group they share therefore counts how many further paper metrics must be held fixed before one determines the other. V and R sit in the group {V, I, R}: fix the current, and resistance alone settles voltage. V and t sit only in groups of size 4, such as {V, R, Q, t}: resistance and charge must both be fixed before time settles voltage. The smaller the group, the more direct the dependence. A group of size 3 is a law linking the two paper metrics through one other; a group of size 4 is a chain through a middle paper metric that neither of them names. Size measures how direct a dependence is, not how large its effect is. It does not say how much voltage changes when time changes, only how many other paper metrics stand between them.

Why dependence rather than co-occurrence. A simpler rule would link two paper metrics whenever they appear in the same equation. That rule measures how the author wrote the paper, not what the paper commits to. It misses links that run through a third paper metric: voltage and time never share an equation above, yet the equations link them through current. It also counts a link more than once when the author restates it, as equation (3) restates the links among current, resistance and power. Two authors stating the same physics in different equations would get different counts. Minimal linked groups avoid both problems, because they depend only on what the equations jointly imply.

Statements that are not equalities. The mechanical test works only on algebraic equalities. Much of a quantum-computing paper is something else: inequalities such as an error bound, big-O statements about cost, expectation values, and statements about operators that do not commute. Algebra cannot eliminate a paper metric from these, so they yield no linked groups. Instead a language model reads each such statement and records, for each pair of named metrics in it, whether the statement makes one depend on the other. It quotes the statement, so that a person can check every call. These links enter the pair-level reading, linked or not, like any other link. They carry no group size, however, so the two measures of degree, group size and rank, cover the algebraic equalities only. Every count keeps the two kinds of link apart. Mechanical tests exist for some of these forms, cylindrical algebraic decomposition for polynomial inequalities and noncommutative Gröbner bases for operator statements, but none is known for big-O statements, and the method does not use them.

Worked example for prose: one blog sentence

Take the sentence from Google's 2019 post quoted above. Four steps run on it, this time on the blog side:

  1. Metrics by frame (iii, discerned). The sentence has two measurement heads, "number of qubits" and "number of gate cycles". They give the metrics qubit count and depth. It also has a scaling frame, "becomes exponentially more difficult as … grow". That frame gives the metric that scales: the difficulty of classical simulation, which is the metric classical simulation time. Nothing in the sentence is a value, so nothing is dropped.
  2. Metrics by alias (iii, recovered). The paper's alias table has n for the number of qubits, m for the number of cycles, and classical cost among the names for its simulation-time paper metric. All three blog metrics are therefore recovered as well as discerned. Each node records both routes and the quote.
  3. Edge (iv). One connective links the metrics: as qubits and depth grow, classical difficulty grows. That gives two edges, qubit count to classical simulation time and depth to classical simulation time. Both point at the same target, and both carry this sentence as their quote.
  4. Preimages. Each recovered blog metric points back at the paper metrics it matched; that set is its preimage, defined fully in the next section. Qubit count maps to {n}, depth to {m}, and classical simulation time to {classical cost}. The two edges therefore imply two paper pairs, n with classical cost and m with classical cost. The next section looks these pairs up in the paper.

The parentheticals "(width)" and "(depth)" are the blog's own glosses on the paper's names, and they are what makes step 2 easy here. A blog that said "bigger circuits are harder to simulate" would discern nothing, because it has no frame. It would recover nothing, because it uses no alias. Hence it would contribute no edge.

Putting both together: checking the blog against the paper

The last step asks two plain questions. When the blog links two blog metrics, does the paper link the paper metrics they stand for? And when the paper links two paper metrics, does the blog pass that link on?

To ask them, each blog metric is first matched to the paper metric it stands for. A blog metric can be found in two ways, as (iii) describes: the blog uses one of the paper's own names for a paper metric, or it signals a measurement in its own wording. When the blog uses the paper's name, the metric points back at that paper metric. In Google's sentence above, "number of qubits" is the paper's own name for the paper metric it writes as n, so the blog metric qubit count points back at n. The set of paper metrics a blog metric points back at is its preimage: the preimage of qubit count is {n}. Read the other way, qubit count is n's blog image: the blog metric that stands for n. A blog metric found only from the blog's own wording, with none of the paper's names, points back at nothing, so its preimage is empty. A blog metric whose preimage holds two paper metrics has collapsed them: the blog has one blog metric where the paper keeps two apart.

A running example makes this concrete. Imagine a blog post about the electricity paper. It talks about current, power, charge and temperature, and it makes one link: "power rises with current". Current, power and charge are the paper's own names for I, P and Q, so these three blog metrics match them. Temperature appears nowhere in the paper, so it matches no paper metric. The blog never mentions voltage, resistance or time.

Every pair of paper metrics then gets two yes-or-no answers. Does the blog link the pair? It does when one of its edges from (iv) joins the two blog metrics that match them. "Power rises with current" links I and P. Does the paper link the pair? It does when the two paper metrics sit together in one of the paper's minimal linked groups from (ii), or when a prose link from (ii) joins them in the sections switched on. The two answers give each pair one of five statuses. How to read the table: ✓ means yes, ✗ means no.

The blog's second sentence, "heat limits power", cannot be checked at all. Temperature matches no paper metric, so there is no pair of paper metrics to look up.

Four more numbers sit beside the statuses:

  • Orphans are paper metrics with no blog image: no blog metric stands for them. In the example they are V, R and t.
  • Collapsed pairs are two paper metrics that the blog folds into one blog metric. Each is reported with whether the paper links them. The example has none.
  • The grounding ratio of a blog edge is the share of the pairs it links that the paper also links. "Power rises with current" links one pair, I and P, and the paper links it too, so its ratio is 1. On the map, this ratio sets how thick the blog's arc is drawn.
  • Directness travels with every link that comes from the paper's equations: the size of the smallest linked group the pair sits in. I and P sit in {V, I, P}, a group of 3, so the blog's link is backed by a direct law. A link backed only by a group of 4 rests on a longer chain.
