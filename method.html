1. Overview

Skew is measured by comparing a paper with the blog written about it. Two kinds of content are taken from each text, and the blog's version is then checked against the paper's. The first is the metrics: what a text names as measurable, such as the number of qubits or the time a classical computer needs to simulate a circuit. The second is the links a text states between those metrics, such as "Fidelity falls as depth grows". Two texts, with two kinds of content taken from each, make four extractions. Each extraction has its own rule:

MetricsLinks between metrics
Paper2i. Named paper metrics: anything the paper names by symbol or phrase in its abstract or a numbered result, never in an appendix. Mechanical.2ii. The equations are read as constraints and reduced to the minimal linked groups they entail; bounds and operator statements are coded by a language model; prose links use rule 2iv. A statement of independence is not a link. A metric named in a condition is linked to each metric of the pair it conditions.
Blog2iii. Metrics: a dimension noun, never a value. Two overlapping kinds: recovered through the paper's alias table, or discerned from a measurement frame in the text. Coded by a language model with the paper closed; human raters code a sample independently to set the standard.2iv. An explicit connective in one sentence between two metrics. Coded by a language model with the sentence quoted; the same rule reads the paper's prose.

Section 2, Measurement, sets out the four rules in turn (2i to 2iv), and section 3 works them through on an invented example. Section 4 puts the two sides together. It shows how a link in the blog is looked up in the paper, and the four tables the Map tab reports: the status of each paper link (Table 1), per-metric coverage (Table 2), salience (Table 3) and selective simplification (Table 4).

2. Measurement

2i. Metrics in a paper

A paper metric is anything the paper gives a name to and treats as measurable. A paper names a paper metric in one of two ways, and both count:

KindWhat it looks likeExamples
SymbolA letter or expression the paper defines and uses in its mathematics.n (number of qubits), t (simulation time), FXEB (fidelity)
PhraseA metric the paper names only in words, without ever giving it a symbol."gate complexity", "classical cost", "quantum volume"

Not every name in the paper is taken. Only names in certain places count, so that the list is the paper's own account of what it measures, not a record of everything it mentions. Those places are:

  1. the abstract;
  2. every numbered result statement, meaning every theorem, lemma, corollary or proposition, and every numbered equation;
  3. for an experimental paper, also the introduction and main text, because such a paper states its results in prose rather than in theorems. Paper metrics found only there are tagged with that scope, so every count can be read with them or without them.

A fixed list excludes two kinds of name: dummy indices, such as the i in a sum, and operator names. Nothing else is filtered out. If the paper names something in one of those places, it counts as a paper metric, whatever its importance.

Each paper metric is recorded with two things. The first is its first naming: the place and the sentence where the paper first names it, for instance "abstract (53 qubits); Eq. (1), n is the number of qubits". The second is its alias table. A paper often calls one paper metric by several names, and the alias table lists them all: every symbol and phrase the paper uses for that metric, each with a quote. "Gate count", "gate complexity" and the lower-bound symbol g, for example, are one paper metric with three aliases. The alias table is a lookup and nothing more. No count depends on how a paper metric's aliases are grouped. Its one job is to be the list the blog is checked against: when a blog uses one of these names, the blog metric is matched to this paper metric.

One name, one metric (2026-10-31). Whether two names refer to the same paper metric is not left to judgement. Every distinct wording the paper uses is its own metric, with two exceptions, both mechanical. A variant of one wording is the same name: plural or singular, hyphen or space, capitals, and a symbol together with the phrase the paper defines it by ("the number of qubits n"). A naming sentence joins a second name to a metric, and is quoted: "force, which we refer to with f", "gate error rate, which we refer to as error rate", "denoted by", "we call", "written as". A sentence that relates two names by dependence never joins them: "force depends on power", "scales with", "determines", or a formula such as F = 1 − e, is a link between two metrics, coded in 2ii, and says nothing about naming. So "gate error rate", "error rate" and "fidelity" are three metrics unless the paper names one as another. Whether a reader wants to treat them as one is a reading choice, made afterwards with the metric boundary slider described below, never by the coder. The rule replaced an earlier one under which the coder merged names it judged to mean the same thing; that judgement had quietly folded distinct metrics into one, and because a merge cannot be undone later while a split can always be lumped, the split is what is coded.

The metric boundary: reading several terms as one metric. Because every distinct name is coded as its own metric, a reader who thinks "error rate" and "gate error rate" are one thing can say so, on the page, without any recoding. One metric boundary slider does this. It sits on the Metrics tab of the paper and of the blog and at the top of the map, and all copies move together. At its left end, fine, every term is its own metric, as coded, and a blog metric stands for a paper metric only where the blog uses one of the paper's own names for it. The site opens with the slider midway, at a threshold of 0.75. Moving it toward coarse lowers a similarity threshold, and two things happen at that threshold. Two metrics on the same side whose names score at or above it are read as one. A blog metric with no by-name counterpart is mapped to its most similar paper metric, but only when that pair also scores at or above it. Similarity is semantic: the cosine of sentence embeddings (a local model, all-mpnet-base-v2, run once at build time). It is computed on the names alone: the paper's verbatim names for a metric, and the blog metric's label stripped of any gloss. The sentences around a name are never embedded, because they would carry the wording of the links and so make the measure read dependence. Joins chain: if A is similar enough to B and B to C, the three are read as one. Symbols are never joined: a symbol such as n or t is not a word, so the similarity of its name says nothing about what it measures (a symbol joins a phrase only through a naming sentence in the paper, as set out above). At a coarser boundary the links, the mapping and every count on the three tabs are recomputed on the joined metrics; a joined metric carries a "⊕ n" tag with the number of names it holds. Two things the slider never does: it never reads a link as evidence that two metrics are one (a dependence between force and power is not a reason to call them the same), and it never changes the coded data. It is a view, and its setting is part of any count read off the page.

The paper is coded with the blog closed. Every name in the alias table must be a name the paper itself uses: each carries a verbatim sentence of the paper in which it occurs, and a check that runs with every build refuses to publish if any name has no such sentence, if any quoted sentence cannot be found in the paper, or if any link names a metric that does not exist. The coder that reads the paper is given the paper text and the rules and nothing else, so no word from a blog can enter the paper's alias table. The blog is coded the same way, with the paper closed. Only afterwards, with both codings frozen, are the two put side by side to decide which paper metric each blog metric stands for. This order was adopted on 2026-10-30 after an audit found blog words in the alias tables of four pairs that had been coded with both texts open; everything was re-derived under the new order. Since 2026-10-31 the coding runs through a standalone tool (skewcoder) that enforces the order mechanically. Each coder runs in a folder holding only the text it may read. A paper's appendices and supplementary material are cut from its text before any step reads it, so nothing found only there is coded. The metrics coder must find every metric first: it is handed a mechanically generated list of candidate phrases (every phrase with a measurement word such as cost, error, rate, time, depth or "number of") and must code each one or say why it is not a metric, and the check fails if any is left unaccounted for; it may never merge two wordings because they seem to mean the same thing. Metrics are then frozen, and links are coded in a separate pass that may only join metrics already on the list. A link whose end is not on the list is reported as a missing metric, a separate pass decides whether to add it, and the links pass runs again; the links coder never adds a metric itself. Every run records the model, the rule version and the checks it passed.

2ii. Links in a paper

The paper's equations are read for dependence. Two paper metrics are dependent when the paper's equations, taken together, mean that one cannot change without the other. Dependence belongs to the system the equations describe. It is therefore the same however the equations are written or arranged.

The procedure uses nothing outside the paper. It has five rules:

  1. Every equation is a constraint, definitions included. A definition the paper states, such as v = ds/dt, is a relationship like any other. A definition the paper does not state is not used.
  2. Redundancy is removed. An equation that follows from the equations already listed adds nothing. It may be a rearrangement, a substitution, a special case or a derivation. For algebraic equalities the test is mechanical: Gröbner-basis ideal membership. The idea is the one behind long division. A set of equations can be rewritten into a standard form, called its Gröbner basis, in which every consequence of the set shows up as something that divides cleanly. Dividing a candidate equation by that basis then works like dividing a number by a list of factors: a zero remainder means the candidate is a combination of the others and says nothing new, and a non-zero remainder is exactly the part of it the others do not already contain. The rewriting is done by a standard computer-algebra routine, Buchberger's algorithm, and gives the same answer whatever order the equations are written in. The number of equations that survive is the paper's count of independent relationships. That number is its rank.
  3. Links are read off as minimal linked groups. A linked group is a set of paper metrics in which knowing all but one leaves the last, under the surviving equations, only one possible value. A linked group is minimal when no smaller set inside it is already linked. Two paper metrics are linked when they sit together in a minimal linked group. No rewriting of the equations changes the minimal linked groups. In matroid theory, a minimal linked group is called a circuit.
  4. A statement of independence is not a link, and the metrics a condition names join the links. A sentence or theorem that asserts a dependence is a link. One that denies it is recorded with its quote but is not a link. Papers do say this outright (the Trotter paper: "conditions under which the cost of Trotter steps does not explicitly scale with Γ"), and such a statement is treated the same as never linking the two: either way, a blog that links the pair claims something the paper does not support, and that is the one thing the measure is built to catch. When a link, or a denial, holds only under a stated condition, the condition is recorded with its quote, and each metric the condition names is linked to each of A and B: it conditions their interdependence. Two metrics named in the same condition are not linked to each other by this rule. The condition is taken from the sentence, the theorem hypothesis, or a case split in the equation, never from the paper as a whole.
  5. What the algebra cannot reach is coded by a language model. Most of a quantum-computing paper consists of such statements: inequalities, big-O bounds, expectation values and non-commuting operator statements. For each one a language model gives a reading, dependent or not, and quotes the statement, exactly as for a prose link, so that a person can check every call. Every count keeps the mechanical part and the model-coded part separable.

Showing the working for an equation. A metric with a symbol is visibly in the equation that links it. A metric named only in words, such as Trotter error, sits in an equation only because a nearby sentence attaches the name to it ("which implies the Trotter error bound", followed by the bound). That sentence is recorded with the equation and shown beside it, so a reader can see why the metric was counted there. It records the reasoning; it does not change which metrics an equation links.

Prose links. A paper also links paper metrics in its sentences, and these sentences are read with the connective rule of 2iv. A connective is a word or phrase that states how one metric bears on another: depends on, scales with, limited by, requires, as X grows Y falls, determines, because of. A sentence that links two of the paper's named metrics with an explicit connective is a prose link. Each prose link carries its connective, its section, its scope, the quote, and one more field. A sentence that denies a dependence ("does not scale with", "as opposed to", "independent of") is kept as a statement of independence, with its quote, and is not a link. The condition field, when the sentence or the statement it belongs to names one, is the regime under which the link holds: a range on a paper metric such as α ≥ 2, or a hypothesis such as low rank of the coefficient blocks. A condition is recorded only with the words that establish it and where they sit, so that a reader can check the regime, never from the coder's reading of the paper as a whole. The same field sits on every equation statement: a theorem's hypotheses are its condition, cited to the theorem. The default scope is the abstract and introduction. The preliminaries and the body are coded as well, and can be switched on. Prose links are coded by a language model and are the subjective part of the paper side. Every count keeps them separable from the equation layer, so a reader can always see which links rest on a sentence and which rest on an equation.

Three things are reported:

  • For a pair of paper metrics, a reading per statement: linked or independent, each with its condition if one is stated. The same pair can be linked in one regime and independent in another; the Trotter paper states that cost scales with the number of terms Γ under a sequential implementation, and does not under its structure conditions.
  • For the degree of a link, two measures of groups. The first is the size of the smallest linked group the pair sits in, since a law among two paper metrics is a tighter link than a law among six. The second is the rank of the paper.
  • Beside each pair, the number of places in the paper that state it.

The mathematics is standard (Buchberger's Gröbner bases; Cox, Little and O'Shea's textbook treatment of ideal membership).

2iii. Metrics in a blog

A blog metric is a dimension: something that can be measured, such as circuit depth, gate fidelity, qubit count or classical simulation time. The metric is the dimension itself, never a value on it. "53 qubits" is a value; qubit count is the metric. The values a blog reports are stored with the metric's mentions, not as metrics of their own. A named target level on a metric is a milestone and is recorded as one. Quantum supremacy, for example, is a milestone on classical simulation time.

A metric enters the blog's list by either of two routes, recovered or discerned; the third row of the table says how a blog metric that matches no paper name can still be joined to one by the reader. The routes overlap, so the same metric can be found by both:

Only the reader can join it to a paper metric, with the metric boundary described in 2i, which maps it to its most similar paper metric once their names are similar enough. No coder infers such a mapping.Both codings, and the metric boundary setting"operations" joins the paper's gate count only if the slider is set coarse enough

A measurement frame is a pattern of words that signals that a sentence reports a measurement. The table lists the frames. Beside each is its lift, which shows how strongly the frame marks a metric sentence in the team's hand-coded set (lift = how much more often the frame appears in sentences coded as metric than elsewhere):

A third, mechanical check runs beside the two routes: a list of dimension names the team compiled, such as fidelity, circuit depth, quantum volume and CLOPS, is matched with loosened spellings, and any sentence it hits that carries no blog metric yet is read again. Topic words such as classical, qubit or scale are not in the list, because they mark a subject rather than a dimension. Future tense and hedges work the other way. Words such as "will", "could" and "aims to" mark a sentence as not about a measurement. Some sentences explain a metric by a claim that is not itself about a metric, such as "random circuits have no exploitable structure". Such a sentence produces no node; it is kept as an audit note only. Each metric is one node, mirroring the paper side. Each node lists every mention of its metric, with the quote and the route that found it. The blog, too, may call one metric by several terms; the metric boundary set out in 2i reads blog metrics as one in the same way, by the semantic similarity of their names.

Human raters. People do this extraction. In the Metric Coder, each rater reads a blog and highlights every metric, privately. The highlighted spans are exported with character offsets, so two raters' choices can be compared exactly. Inter-rater reliability, the agreement among the human raters, sets the standard. A language-model pass must match that standard before it is used on the rest of the corpus.

2iv. Links in a blog

A blog states a link when one sentence connects two blog metrics with an explicit connective. Such a link is an edge between the two metrics. "Fidelity falls as depth grows" is an edge from depth to fidelity. Blog edges are coded by a language model. The rule is narrow so that any two readers of the same sentence, human or model, make the same call.

An edge must have three things:

  1. Both ends are metrics already in the blog's list under 2iii. A sentence whose other end is not a metric is kept as an audit note.
  2. A connective in the sentence: depends on, scales with, limited by, requires, as X grows Y falls, the more X the less Y, determines, because of. Two metrics mentioned in one sentence with no connective between them are not an edge.
  3. The sentence itself, quoted and stored with the edge, so that the call can be checked.

Each edge points the way the sentence states it, from A to B, and carries the same fields as a paper link. A blog sentence that denies a dependence ("cost does not scale with the number of terms") is kept as a statement of independence and is not an edge. A condition the sentence states ("when the target Hamiltonian is structured") is recorded with the words that establish it, and the blog metric it names is linked to each end of the edge, as on the paper side. Each edge also keeps a type assigned by the language-model coder: faithful, conditions dropped, collapsed, misattributed or invented. The type is a label only; no count uses it.

The paper's prose links in 2ii are read with this same rule.

Three sentences show the rule at work. The first two come from IBM's 2019 post on Google's supremacy paper. "an ideal simulation of the same task can be performed on a classical system in 2.5 days and with far greater fidelity" links nothing: it names two metrics but has no connective. "with additional refinements the classical cost of the simulation can be further reduced" links nothing either: it names only one metric. Google's own post, by contrast, says "finding the most likely bitstrings for a random quantum circuit on a classical computer becomes exponentially more difficult as the number of qubits (width) and number of gate cycles (depth) grow". That sentence gives two edges, qubit count to classical simulation time and depth to classical simulation time, with the connective "becomes exponentially more difficult as … grow".

3. Worked example

3i. A short paper on electricity

The paper-side rules can be followed step by step on a small paper about electricity. Imagine a paper with four equations. Its abstract names voltage V, current I, resistance R, power P, charge Q and time t, and adds one sentence: "For a superconducting wire, at temperature T below the critical temperature T_c, the voltage drop is independent of the current." Its numbered equations are:

(1) V = IR     (2) P = IV     (3) P = I²R     (4) Q = It

Step 1, metrics 2i. There are seven named metrics: V, I, R, P, Q and t, each named in the abstract and used in a numbered equation, and the temperature T, named only in the sentence on superconductors.

Step 2, redundancy (2ii, rule 2). Add the equations one at a time, and ask whether each one says something new. (1) and (2) do. (3) does not: substitute V = IR into P = IV and the result is P = I²R. Hence (1) and (2) already imply (3), and (3) adds nothing. (4) does say something new. The paper therefore states 3 independent relationships, not 4.

Step 3, minimal linked groups (2ii, rule 3). A set of paper metrics is linked when knowing all of its members but one leaves the last with only one possible value. This must hold whatever values the paper metrics outside the set take. {V, I, R} is linked: if I = 2 and R = 3, then V must be 6. {V, Q} is not linked, because knowing V leaves Q open. A link can run through a paper metric outside the set. In {V, R, Q, t}, V and R fix the current I, and I and t then fix Q. A linked set is minimal when every member is needed: remove any one member and what is left is no longer linked. {V, I, R, Q} is linked, but it is not minimal. Remove Q and {V, I, R} is still linked, so Q plays no part. {V, I, R} is minimal: remove any one of the three and the other two are free. The three surviving equations give eight minimal linked groups. Each is listed with the single relation that links it:

Minimal linked groupRelation the paper entailsSizeWritten in the paper?
{V, I, R}V = IR3✓ equation (1)
{V, I, P}P = IV3✓ equation (2)
{I, R, P}P = I²R3✓ equation (3), implied by (1) and (2)
{V, R, P}V² = PR3✗ never written
{I, Q, t}Q = It3✓ equation (4)
{V, R, Q, t}Vt = QR4✗ never written
{V, P, Q, t}QV = Pt4✗ never written
{R, P, Q, t}Q²R = Pt²4✗ never written

How to read the last column: ✓ means the relation appears among the paper's numbered equations, ✗ means it does not. Two rows deserve a note. The group {I, R, P} is equation (3), which the paper wrote even though (1) and (2) already imply it. The group {V, R, P}, linked by V² = PR, appears nowhere in the paper, yet (1) and (2) imply it as well. The same holds for the three groups of size 4. Four of the eight minimal linked groups are therefore relationships the paper never wrote down but is committed to by the equations it did write.

Why minimal linked groups are measured. They are what the equations commit the paper to, rather than what the author chose to write. The paper above could have printed P = I²R or left it out, and could have added V² = PR. Either way the eight groups stay the same, because they follow from the three independent equations. A measure built on them therefore does not reward an author who restates a law, and does not miss a link that runs through a third paper metric. The groups are also what a blog claim is checked against. When a blog says two metrics move together, the question is whether the paper's equations link the corresponding paper metrics, and the minimal linked groups answer it.

What the size of a group says about the strength of a dependence. In a minimal linked group of size k, one member is fixed once the other k − 1 are known. For two paper metrics, the size of the smallest group they share therefore counts how many further paper metrics must be held fixed before one determines the other. V and R sit in the group {V, I, R}: fix the current, and resistance alone settles voltage. V and t sit only in groups of size 4, such as {V, R, Q, t}: resistance and charge must both be fixed before time settles voltage. The smaller the group, the more direct the dependence. A group of size 3 is a law linking the two paper metrics through one other; a group of size 4 is a chain through a middle paper metric that neither of them names. Size measures how direct a dependence is, not how large its effect is. It does not say how much voltage changes when time changes, only how many other paper metrics stand between them.

Why dependence rather than co-occurrence. A simpler rule would link two paper metrics whenever they appear in the same equation. That rule measures how the author wrote the paper, not what the paper commits to. It misses links that run through a third paper metric: voltage and time never share an equation above, yet the equations link them through current. It also counts a link more than once when the author restates it, as equation (3) restates the links among current, resistance and power. Two authors stating the same physics in different equations would get different counts. Minimal linked groups avoid both problems, because they depend only on what the equations jointly imply.

Statements that are not equalities. The mechanical test works only on algebraic equalities. Much of a quantum-computing paper is something else: inequalities such as an error bound, big-O statements about cost, expectation values, and statements about operators that do not commute. Algebra cannot eliminate a paper metric from these, so they yield no linked groups. Instead a language model reads each such statement and records, for each pair of named metrics in it, whether the statement makes one depend on the other. It quotes the statement, so that a person can check every call. These links enter the pair-level reading, linked or not, like any other link. They carry no group size, however, so the two measures of degree, group size and rank, cover the algebraic equalities only. Every count keeps the two kinds of link apart. Mechanical tests exist for some of these forms, cylindrical algebraic decomposition for polynomial inequalities and noncommutative Gröbner bases for operator statements, but none is known for big-O statements, and the method does not use them.

Step 4, a statement of independence and its condition (2ii, rule 5). The sentence on superconductors joins two paper metrics with the connective "independent of". It denies the dependence, so it is recorded as a statement of independence, not as a link; V and I stay linked by equation (1). It holds under a stated condition, "at temperature T below the critical temperature T_c", recorded with those words. By rule 5 the metric the condition names, T, is linked to each of V and I: T conditions their interdependence, and V–T and I–T are the links drawn. The paper never writes an equation with T in it; it is committed to these links by the condition it states, as it is committed to V² = PR by equations (1) and (2).

3ii. A short blog post about the electricity paper

The blog-side rules can be followed on an invented blog post about the same electricity paper. The post is three sentences long:

(1) Our new paper shows that the power a device draws rises with the current through it.
(2) At higher temperatures, heat limits the power a device can safely draw.
(3) The paper also reports how much charge each device delivered.
  1. Blog metrics by frame (2iii, discerned). Sentence (1) has a scaling frame, "rises with", which gives two blog metrics: power, the one that rises, and current, the one it rises with. Sentence (2) has a degree-modified property, "higher temperatures", which gives temperature. Sentence (3) has a measurement head, "how much charge", which gives charge. No sentence reports a value, so nothing is set aside.
  2. Blog metrics by alias (2iii, recovered). "Power", "current" and "charge" are the paper's own names for P, I and Q. These three blog metrics are therefore recovered as well as discerned. "Temperature" is the paper's own name for T, named in its sentence on superconductors, so it too is recovered.
  3. Links 2iv. Sentence (1) links current to power with the connective "rises with". Sentence (2) links temperature to power with the connective "limits". Sentence (3) names charge but has no connective, so it gives no link.
  4. Preimages. Each blog metric points back at the paper metrics it stands for; that set is its preimage, explained fully in the next section. Current maps to {I}, power to {P} and charge to {Q}. Temperature maps to {T}. The link from current to power gives the pair I and P to look up; the link from temperature to power gives T and P.

4. Putting both together: checking the blog against the paper

The last step asks two plain questions. When the blog links two blog metrics, does the paper link the paper metrics they stand for? And when the paper links two paper metrics, does the blog pass that link on?

To ask them, each blog metric is first matched to the paper metric it stands for. A blog metric can be found in two ways, as 2iii describes: the blog uses one of the paper's own names for a paper metric, or it signals a measurement in its own wording. When the blog uses the paper's name, the metric points back at that paper metric. In the invented post, "current" is the paper's own name for the paper metric it writes as I, so the blog metric current points back at I. The set of paper metrics a blog metric points back at is its preimage: the preimage of current is {I}. Read the other way, current is I's blog image: the blog metric that stands for I. A blog metric found only from the blog's own wording, with none of the paper's names, points back at nothing, so its preimage is empty. A blog metric whose preimage holds two paper metrics has collapsed them: the blog has one blog metric where the paper keeps two apart.

The invented post from the worked example runs through this section. Its blog metrics are current, power, temperature and charge, and it makes two links: current to power, and temperature to power. They stand for the paper metrics I, P, T and Q. The post never mentions voltage, resistance or time.

Every pair of paper metrics then gets three answers. Does the blog have both metrics? It does when a blog metric stands for each of the two. Does the blog link the pair? It does when one of its edges from 2iv joins the two blog metrics that match them, or when one of them is named in a condition on an edge of the other. The link from current to power covers I and P. Does the paper link the pair? It does when the two paper metrics sit together in one of the paper's minimal linked groups from 2ii, when a prose link from 2ii joins them in the sections switched on, or when one conditions a link of the other. A link that came from a condition counts like any other link. The three answers give each pair one of five statuses.

When one blog word stands for several paper metrics. The map draws such a metric as a diamond, on either side. The coder records why. Sometimes the blog has bundled several paper quantities into one name and names each of them (a size made of electrons and orbitals). Sometimes the blog's term is its own and the text does not settle which paper metric it means; then the mapping is unsure, and its lines in the middle of the map are dashed. Whether the paper links the metrics behind the word is a separate question, answered on the paper side.

Per-metric coverage (Table 2). The same three questions can be asked from the side of one paper metric. Count the partners the paper links it to, how many of those partners the blog names, and among those how many of the links the blog states. A blog that simplifies everything alike gives every metric about the same share. A blog that talks up some dimensions gives those metrics a high share and leaves others, whose partners it also names, with none. The Map tab lists this per metric together with the metric's betweenness in the paper's link graph, so a dropped link on a hub can be told from one on a leaf.

Salience: how much the blog talks about each metric (Table 3). Each paper metric gets three shares, all of which sum to 1 across metrics. A is its share of the paper's sentences: the distinct sentences that use any of its names, together with its coded sources (each numbered equation it is in, each prose link or condition naming it, each sentence attaching it to an equation), so a symbol such as n is counted by its sources, not by every occurrence of the letter. B is its share of the blog's sentences: the distinct sentences that are a coded mention of a blog metric standing for it, or that use the words that blog metric was matched on. C is its PageRank in the paper's link graph (damping 0.85), a measure of how central the paper makes it. Two ratios follow: mention skew = B ÷ A and centrality skew = B ÷ C. Above 1, the blog gives the metric more of its attention than the paper does; below 1, less. In the invented post, the four blog metrics take five sentence-mentions in all (current in sentence 1, power in 1 and 2, temperature in 2, charge in 3), so B for power is 2/5. In the electricity paper, taking the abstract to name its six metrics in one sentence, the seven metrics take 21 sentence-mentions and sources in all, of which P has 3 (the abstract and equations (2) and (3)), so A is 1/7. Mention skew for power is (2/5) ÷ (1/7) = ×2.8: the post gives power almost three times the attention the paper does.

Selective simplification: which metrics keep their links (Table 4). A blog drops most of a paper's links; the question is whether it drops them evenly. The unit is the metric, because a link can only appear in the blog if the blog names its metrics. For each paper metric the blog names, D is the number of links the paper makes for it, and coverage = carried ÷ D is the share of them the blog states. The blog's baseline p̄ is the average coverage over the metrics it names, each metric counting once: the share of its links a typical metric keeps. Selectivity = coverage ÷ p̄. A blog that simplifies evenly puts every metric near ×1; a blog that keeps all five links of one metric and one of the three links of another puts the first well above and the second well below. An evenness test asks whether the spread could be chance: if every named metric kept each of its links with the same chance p̄, how often would the coverage of the metrics be at least as spread out as it is? The answer is found by drawing 2,000 such blogs and is reported as a p-value. A metric the blog never names reads NA in every link cell and stays out of p̄; Table 3 covers it. In the invented post, the blog names current, power, charge and temperature. The paper links current I to V, R, P, Q, t and T (six links) and the post carries one, to P: coverage 1/6. Power P is linked to V, I, R, Q and t (five) and the post carries one: 1/5. Charge Q is linked to I, t, V, R and P (five) and temperature T to V and I (two); the post carries none of these. Its heat-limits-power link is ungrounded and not counted. So p̄ = (1/6 + 1/5 + 0 + 0) ÷ 4 ≈ 0.09, and selectivity is ×1.8 for current, ×2.2 for power and ×0 for charge and temperature: the post keeps links around the two metrics of its first sentence and none around the others.

What the tool shows as evidence for each mapping. Beside every paper metric in a blog metric's preimage, the Blog tab states how the two are tied, read off the blog text by rule rather than by judgment. Same term: the blog uses one of the paper's own names for the metric (matched loosely: case, hyphen or space, singular or plural), and the passages are listed. Explicit: one sentence of the blog holds two of the paper's names for the metric that are not variants of one another, as in "number of gate cycles (depth)"; the sentence is quoted so the reader can see whether the blog is stating the equivalence itself, because the rule only finds the candidate. By similarity: none of the paper's names occurs in the blog text, and the mapping exists only because the metric boundary is set coarse enough to join the two names. None: the preimage is empty. In the invented post, current is "same term" because the paper also writes "current"; had the post said only "the flow through the wire", current would stand for nothing at the fine end of the slider. These labels describe the audit trail; no count uses them.

Where a condition comes from. A condition is never inferred from the paper as a whole. It is taken from one of three places, each cited with the quote: the sentence itself ("for α ≥ 2", "in the regime of strong entanglement"); the hypothesis of the theorem or lemma an equation belongs to ("Assume that the coefficient oracle …", "Assume that the terms pairwise commute"); or a case split written into the equation (α ≥ 1 versus 0 < α < 1). A sentence that denies a dependence is recorded, where it names the regime, with that condition too, and the condition's metrics are linked to the pair in the same way.

A metric named in a condition is linked to the pair it conditions. One rule turns conditions into links. If the paper links A to B under a condition naming C, or denies the link under that condition, then C conditions the A–B link, and the links A–C and B–C are drawn, cited to the sentence that states the condition. The pair A–B keeps its own rows. When a condition names two metrics, each is linked to A and to B, but not to the other: the paper has listed two hypotheses, not stated a dependence between them. The Trotter paper says the cost of a Trotter step scales with the number of terms Γ for a sequential implementation, and does not under its low-rank and coefficient-oracle conditions. The rule links rank to cost per step and to Γ, and coefficient structure to both as well: rank is needed to know which regime applies, Γ is needed inside one of them. No equation in the paper contains both Γ and rank, so the Γ–rank link is derived, never written, in the same way the electricity paper is committed to V² = PR.

Four more numbers sit beside the statuses:

  • Paper metrics with no blog image: no blog metric stands for them. In the example they are V, R and t.
  • Collapsed pairs are two paper metrics that the blog folds into one blog metric. Each is reported with whether the paper links them. The example has none.
  • The grounding ratio of a blog edge is the share of the pairs it links that the paper also links. The link from current to power covers one pair, I and P, and the paper links it too, so its ratio is 1. On the map, a blog arc whose pairs the paper all links is drawn thick and solid, one with some of them linked is drawn thinner, and one with none linked is drawn dashed.
  • Directness travels with every link that comes from the paper's equations: the size of the smallest linked group the pair sits in. I and P sit in {V, I, P}, a group of 3, so the blog's link is backed by a direct law. A link backed only by a group of 4 rests on a longer chain.
