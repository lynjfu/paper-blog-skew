Paper–blog skew: how metrics and interdependencies are extracted

Overview

Skew is measured by extracting the same two things from a paper and from the blog written about it, then checking the blog's version against the paper's: the metrics each one names, and the interdependencies each one states between those metrics. Four extractions, four rules:

Metrics (the units)Interdependencies (the ties)
Paper(iii) Named quantities: anything the paper names by symbol or phrase in its abstract or a numbered result (L6). Mechanical.(iv) Dependence, not co-occurrence: equalities are read as constraints and reduced to the minimal tied groups they entail (L17, 2026-10-28); bounds and operator statements are coded by hand; prose links use rule (ii).
Blog(i) Metrics: a dimension noun, never a value (L12). Two overlapping kinds: recovered through the paper's alias table, or discerned from a measurement frame in the text. Now being handed to human raters in the Metric Coder.(ii) An explicit connective in one sentence between two metrics (L3, extended to the paper's prose by L7b). Hand-coded with the sentence quoted.

What is settled: (i), (ii) and (iii) have been applied to five blog–paper pairs (the Trotter pair, the 2019 supremacy paper against Google's and IBM's posts, the 2023 utility paper against IBM's and Google's posts). What changed on 2026-10-28: (iv) moves from counting which quantities share an equation to computing which quantities the equations make dependent; it is ruled but not yet rebuilt, and will be prototyped on the Trotter paper first. What has not been reviewed yet: the skew measures in the last section, which still run on the old (iv).

(i) Metrics in a blog

A blog metric is a dimension, the thing something is measured on, never the value it takes (L12, 2026-10-27). "Depth" is a metric; "depth 20" is a value on it. A named target level on a metric, such as quantum supremacy on classical simulation time, is a milestone and is recorded as one.

A metric enters the blog's list by either of two routes, which overlap:

Future tense and hedges work the other way: "will", "could", "aims to" mark a sentence as not about a measurement. A sentence that explains a metric by a non-metric claim ("random circuits have no exploitable structure") produces no node; it is kept as an audit note only. One node per metric, mirroring the paper side, each node listing every mention with its quote and which route found it.

Human raters. This is the extraction being handed to people. The Metric Coder (live at https://146-190-251-6.sslip.io) offers 123 blogs that a hand check confirmed are about a paper; each rater highlights every metric, privately, and the spans are exported with character offsets so two raters' choices can be compared exactly. The plan, as in the LRAW and expd3 projects: establish inter-rater reliability among the human raters, then run a language model on the same blogs and keep it only if it matches the human level, and warrant the automated pass from there.

(ii) Interdependencies in a blog, and in the paper's prose

A blog states an interdependency when one sentence ties two metrics with an explicit connective (L3). "Fidelity falls as depth grows" is an edge from depth to fidelity. Two sentences that each mention a metric, with no connective, are not an edge. The rule is deliberately narrow so that two coders reading the same sentence make the same call.

What an edge must have:

  1. Both ends are metrics already in the blog's list under (i). No node is created only to give an edge an end (L12); if one end is not a metric, the sentence becomes an audit note.
  2. A connective in the sentence: depends on, scales with, limited by, requires, as X grows Y falls, the more X the less Y, determines, because of. Co-mention without a connective is not enough.
  3. The sentence, quoted, stored with the edge, so the call can be checked.

Each edge is directed as the sentence states it (A to B) and keeps a hand-coded type (faithful, conditions dropped, collapsed, misattributed, invented) as a label only; no count uses the type. A negated statement ("does not depend on") is kept with its polarity and is off by default in every view.

The same rule reads the paper's prose (L7b): a sentence of the paper that ties two of its named quantities with an explicit connective is a prose link, carrying the connective, the section, the scope and the quote. The default scope is the abstract and introduction (L11); the preliminaries and the body are coded and can be switched on (C15). Prose links are the subjective part of the paper side and are kept separable from the equation layer in every count, so a reader can always see which ties rest on a sentence and which on an equation.

Worked instance (IBM's 2019 post on Google's supremacy paper): "an ideal simulation of the same task can be performed on a classical system in 2.5 days and with far greater fidelity" ties nothing: two metrics, no connective. "with additional refinements the classical cost of the simulation can be further reduced" ties nothing either: one metric. Whereas Google's own post says "finding the most likely bitstrings for a random quantum circuit on a classical computer becomes exponentially more difficult as the number of qubits (width) and number of gate cycles (depth) grow", which is two edges, qubit count to classical simulation time and depth to classical simulation time, connective "becomes exponentially more difficult as … grow".

(iii) Metrics in a paper

A paper metric is a named quantity: anything the paper names, by symbol or by phrase, in its abstract or in a numbered result statement, a theorem, lemma, corollary or numbered equation (L6). The paper's own name is kept. Dummy indices and operator names are excluded by a stated list. No judgement about what is "core" is applied; if the paper names it in those places, it is in.

Each quantity carries an alias table (C8): every symbol and phrase the paper uses for it, each with a quote and its location. "Gate count", "gate complexity" and the lower-bound symbol g are one quantity with three aliases (L10). The aliases are a lookup, nothing more: no count depends on how a quantity's aliases are grouped (L8). The table is what route (i)-recovered reads the blog against.

Where a paper has few numbered results, the strict rule leaves out quantities the blogs talk about. For experimental papers the quantities named only in the body are included and tagged with their scope, so they can be excluded by rule (C18; Lyn's ruling on it is pending):

PaperStrict L6Body-named (C18)Total
Arute et al. 2019 (supremacy)8917
Kim et al. 2023 (utility)131730

The extraction is mechanical once the scope is fixed: the coder lists what the paper names where the rule says to look, and every entry cites its first naming. The one judgement is whether two names are the same quantity, which the alias table records so a second coder can disagree at a specific line.

(iv) Interdependencies in a paper

The paper's equations are read for dependence, not co-occurrence (L17, 2026-10-28). Two quantities co-occur when they share an equation on the page. They are dependent when the paper's equations, taken together, mean that one cannot change without the other. The two come apart: F = ma and F = kt never put a and t on the same page, yet tie them exactly as tightly as a and m. Co-occurrence is a fact about the layout; dependence is a fact about the system, and it does not change when an equation is rearranged.

The procedure, using nothing outside the paper:

  1. Every equation is a constraint, definitions included. v = ds/dt counts as a relationship like any other, so whether the paper writes it changes the result, correctly, because we are counting the paper.
  2. Redundancy is removed. An equation that follows from the others already listed adds nothing: a rearrangement, a substitution, a special case, a derivation. For algebraic equalities this test is mechanical (Gröbner-basis ideal membership: divide the candidate by the others; a zero remainder means it is implied). The number that survives is the paper's count of independent relationships, its rank.
  3. Ties are read off as minimal tied groups. A set of quantities is tied if the surviving equations force them to be dependent; a minimal tied group is one with no tied subset. These groups are the paper's interdependencies. They do not change under any rewriting of the equations.
  4. What the algebra cannot reach is coded by hand. Inequalities, big-O bounds, expectation values and non-commuting operator statements, which make up most of a quantum-computing paper, get a dependent-or-not reading from the coder with the statement quoted, exactly as a prose link does. The mechanical and the hand-coded parts stay separable in every count.

What is reported:

  • At the level of a pair: binary. Two quantities are tied or they are not. A graded strength on a pair has no rearrangement-proof meaning, so none is reported.
  • Degree, as two measures of groups: the size of the smallest tied group a pair sits in (a two-quantity law is a tighter tie than a six-quantity one), and the rank of the paper. Neither is labelled with a borrowed name.
  • The interaction table's cell counts, which were the number of equations restating a pair, are relabelled "places the paper restates this". They describe presentation, not dependence.

Rejected on the way: any rule that reads structure off the layout, such as linking only across the equals sign, since V = IR and I = V/R are the same statement.

Status. Ruled, not yet rebuilt. The Trotter paper's 222 equations are currently hand-grouped into 34 statements and ties are counted as co-occurrence within a statement; the prototype of L17 will run on those 34 first and report how many the mechanical test reaches. The mathematics is standard (Buchberger 1965; Cox, Little and O'Shea's textbook); its application to published papers is not, the nearest prior work being a 2024 NLP task that labels which equations in a manuscript derive from which.

Worked example: a short paper on electricity

Imagine a four-equation paper. Its abstract names voltage V, current I, resistance R, power P, charge Q and time t, and its numbered equations are:

(1) V = IR     (2) P = IV     (3) P = I²R     (4) Q = It

Step 1, metrics (iii). Six named quantities: V, I, R, P, Q, t. Each is named in the abstract and appears in a numbered equation.

Step 2, redundancy (iv, rule 2). Add the equations one at a time and ask whether each says something new. (1) and (2) do. (3) does not: substitute V = IR into P = IV and you get P = I²R, so (3) is implied by (1) and (2) and adds nothing. (4) does. The paper states 3 independent relationships, not 4.

Step 3, minimal tied groups (iv, rule 3). Which sets of quantities do the three surviving equations force to move together, with no smaller set inside them that already does? There are eight, each with the single relation that ties it:

Minimal tied groupRelation the paper entailsSize
{V, I, R}V = IR3
{V, I, P}P = IV3
{I, R, P}P = I²R3
{V, R, P}V² = PR3
{I, Q, t}Q = It3
{V, R, Q, t}Vt = QR4
{V, P, Q, t}QV = Pt4
{R, P, Q, t}Q²R = Pt²4

The third and fourth groups are the two rearrangements the paper might or might not have written; they exist either way, because (1) and (2) entail them. The last three groups are relationships the paper never wrote down but which its equations commit it to.

Three things to see. Co-occurrence misses six pairs (the bottom block) that the paper's equations tie, because the tie runs through a third quantity. It counts I–R and I–P twice only because the author chose to write equation (3), a restatement. And under dependence every pair is tied, which is what a connected system looks like; the information is not in the yes/no but in the size of the smallest group (3 for a law the paper wrote, 4 for a tie that runs through a third quantity) and in the rank of 3.

The computation is mechanical. The redundancy test is the Gröbner-basis remainder; a group is tied if eliminating every other quantity from the equations leaves a non-trivial relation among the group alone, and it is minimal if no subset passes the same test. The sympy run behind these tables is recorded in project_log.md for 2026-10-28.

Worked example for prose: one blog sentence

Take the sentence from Google's 2019 post quoted above. The same four steps run on it, on the blog side:

  1. Metrics by frame (i, discerned). Two measurement heads, "number of qubits" and "number of gate cycles", give the metrics qubit count and depth. A scaling frame, "becomes exponentially more difficult as … grow", gives the thing that scales, the difficulty of classical simulation, which is the metric classical simulation time. Nothing in the sentence is a value, so nothing is dropped.
  2. Metrics by alias (i, recovered). The paper's alias table has n for the number of qubits, m for the number of cycles, and classical cost among the names for its simulation-time quantity. All three blog metrics are therefore recovered as well as discerned; each node records both routes and the quote.
  3. Edge (ii). One connective ties them: as qubits and depth grow, classical difficulty grows. Two edges, qubit count to classical simulation time and depth to classical simulation time, both pointing at the same target, both carrying this sentence as their quote.
  4. Preimages. Qubit count maps to {n}, depth to {m}, classical simulation time to {classical cost}. The two edges imply two paper pairs, n with classical cost and m with classical cost, which the next section looks up in the paper.

The parenthetical "(width)" and "(depth)" are the blog's own glosses on the paper's names; they are what makes step 2 easy here. A blog that said "bigger circuits are harder to simulate" would discern nothing (no frame) and recover nothing (no alias), and would contribute no edge, which is the intended behaviour: the rule does not reward vagueness with a tie.

Putting both together: the skew measures as they stand

The two sides meet through a mapping: each blog metric found by route (i)-recovered points at the paper quantities whose aliases it used. That set is the metric's preimage. A discerned-only metric has an empty preimage; a blog metric whose preimage holds two paper quantities has collapsed them into one word.

A blog edge A to B (from (ii)) then implies every pair of paper quantities one can form from pre(A) and pre(B). Each implied pair is looked up in the paper's ties (from (iv), plus the prose links from (ii) under whichever scopes are switched on). That lookup gives every paper pair one of five statuses, and these are the skew measures the site currently reports:

StatusMeaningReads as
representedthe blog implies it and the paper ties itfaithful transmission
ungroundedthe blog implies it and the paper does not tie itthe blog asserts a dependence the paper never states
unlinkedthe paper ties it, both ends have a blog image, the blog never implies ita dependence the blog had the words for and dropped
orphaned endthe paper ties it and at least one end has no blog imagea dependence the blog could not state because it never names one of the quantities
not linkedneither sidenothing to compare

Two more counts sit beside these: orphans, paper quantities with no blog image at all, and collapsed pairs, two paper quantities the blog folds into one metric, which are reported with whether the paper ties them. Each blog edge also gets a grounding ratio, the share of the pairs it implies that the paper ties, which sets the arc's width on the map.

What has not been reviewed. These definitions were built under the old reading of (iv), where "the paper ties it" meant co-occurrence within a grouped statement. Under L17 the natural replacement is "the pair sits in a common minimal tied group", with the group's size carried along, so that a blog edge grounded by a two-quantity law and one grounded only through a four-quantity chain can be told apart. Whether to carry that distinction into the measures, and which of the five statuses the paper should lead with, are open and are the next conversation. No skew number is quoted here for that reason; the figures on the live site are from the old (iv).
