How skew is measured: metrics and interdependencies in a paper and in its blog

Overview

Skew is measured by comparing a paper with the blog written about it. The same two things are taken from each text, and the blog's version is then checked against the paper's. The first is the metrics: the measurable quantities a text names, such as the number of qubits or the time a classical computer needs to simulate a circuit. The second is the interdependencies: the ties a text states between those metrics, such as "Fidelity falls as depth grows". Two texts, with two things taken from each, make four extractions. Each extraction has its own rule:

Metrics (the units)Interdependencies (the ties)
Paper(i) Named quantities: anything the paper names by symbol or phrase in its abstract or a numbered result. Mechanical.(ii) The equations are read as constraints and reduced to the minimal tied groups they entail; bounds and operator statements are coded by hand; prose links use rule (iv).
Blog(iii) Metrics: a dimension noun, never a value. Two overlapping kinds: recovered through the paper's alias table, or discerned from a measurement frame in the text. Coded by several human raters independently.(iv) An explicit connective in one sentence between two metrics. Hand-coded with the sentence quoted; the same rule reads the paper's prose.

Sections (i) to (iv) set out the four rules in turn, and two worked examples follow them. The final section puts the two sides together. It shows how a tie in the blog is looked up in the paper, and the counts that result.

(i) Metrics in a paper

A paper metric is a named quantity: something the paper gives a name to and treats as measurable. A paper names a quantity in one of two ways, and both count:

KindWhat it looks likeExamples
SymbolA letter or expression the paper defines and uses in its mathematics.n (number of qubits), t (simulation time), FXEB (fidelity)
PhraseA quantity the paper names only in words, without ever giving it a symbol."gate complexity", "classical cost", "quantum volume"

Not every name in the paper is taken. Only names in certain places count, so that the list is the paper's own account of what it measures, not a record of everything it mentions. Those places are:

  1. the abstract;
  2. every numbered result statement, meaning every theorem, lemma, corollary or proposition, and every numbered equation;
  3. for an experimental paper, also the introduction and main text, because such a paper states its results in prose rather than in theorems. Quantities found only there are tagged with that scope, so every count can be read with them or without them.

A fixed list excludes two kinds of name: dummy indices, such as the i in a sum, and operator names. Nothing else is filtered out. If the paper names a quantity in one of those places, the quantity is in, whatever its importance.

Each quantity is recorded with two things. The first is its first naming: the place and the sentence where the paper first names it, for instance "abstract (53 qubits); Eq. (1), n is the number of qubits". The second is its alias table. A paper often calls one quantity by several names, and the alias table lists them all: every symbol and phrase the paper uses for that quantity, each with a quote. "Gate count", "gate complexity" and the lower-bound symbol g, for example, are one quantity with three aliases. The alias table is a lookup and nothing more. No count depends on how a quantity's aliases are grouped. Its one job is to serve as the list that route (iii)-recovered reads the blog against.

Once those places are fixed, the extraction is mechanical. One judgement remains: whether two names refer to the same quantity. The language model that does the extraction records each such call in the alias table, so a person can disagree at a specific line.

(ii) Interdependencies in a paper

The paper's equations are read for dependence. Two quantities are dependent when the paper's equations, taken together, mean that one cannot change without the other. Dependence belongs to the system the equations describe. It is therefore the same however the equations are written or arranged.

The procedure uses nothing outside the paper. It has four rules:

  1. Every equation is a constraint, definitions included. A definition the paper states, such as v = ds/dt, is a relationship like any other. A definition the paper does not state is not used.
  2. Redundancy is removed. An equation that follows from the equations already listed adds nothing. It may be a rearrangement, a substitution, a special case or a derivation. For algebraic equalities the test is mechanical: Gröbner-basis ideal membership. The candidate equation is divided by the others, and a zero remainder means the others already imply it. The number of equations that survive is the paper's count of independent relationships. That number is its rank.
  3. Ties are read off as minimal tied groups. A tied group is a set of quantities that the surviving equations force to be dependent. A tied group is minimal when no smaller set inside it is already tied. The minimal tied groups are the paper's interdependencies. No rewriting of the equations changes them.
  4. What the algebra cannot reach is coded by a language model. Most of a quantum-computing paper consists of such statements: inequalities, big-O bounds, expectation values and non-commuting operator statements. For each one a language model gives a reading, dependent or not, and quotes the statement, exactly as for a prose link, so that a person can check every call. Every count keeps the mechanical part and the hand-coded part separable.

Prose links. A paper also ties quantities in its sentences, and these sentences are read with the connective rule of (iv). A connective is a word or phrase that states how one thing bears on another: depends on, scales with, limited by, requires, as X grows Y falls, determines, because of. A sentence that ties two of the paper's named quantities with an explicit connective is a prose link. Each prose link carries its connective, its section, its scope and the quote. The default scope is the abstract and introduction. The preliminaries and the body are coded as well, and can be switched on. Prose links are coded by a language model and are the subjective part of the paper side. Every count keeps them separable from the equation layer, so a reader can always see which ties rest on a sentence and which rest on an equation.

Three things are reported:

  • For a pair of quantities, a binary reading: the two are tied or they are not.
  • For the degree of a tie, two measures of groups. The first is the size of the smallest tied group the pair sits in, since a law among two quantities is a tighter tie than a law among six. The second is the rank of the paper.
  • Beside each pair, the number of places in the paper that state it.

The mathematics is standard (Buchberger's Gröbner bases; Cox, Little and O'Shea's textbook treatment of ideal membership).

(iii) Metrics in a blog

A blog metric is a dimension: a thing that can be measured, such as circuit depth, gate fidelity, qubit count or classical simulation time. The metric is the dimension itself, never a value on it. "53 qubits" is a value; qubit count is the metric. The values a blog reports are stored with the metric's mentions, not as metrics of their own. A named target level on a metric is a milestone and is recorded as one. Quantum supremacy, for example, is a milestone on classical simulation time.

A metric enters the blog's list by either of two routes. The routes overlap, so the same metric can be found by both:

RouteHow it is foundWhat it needsExample
RecoveredThe blog uses one of the paper's own names for a paper quantity. The match goes through the paper's alias table (every symbol and phrase the paper uses for each quantity, with the quote).The paper already coded under (i)"53 qubits" recovers the paper's n
DiscernedA dimension noun sits inside a measurement frame in the blog's own text. The frames were derived by the team from 1,638 hand-coded blog sentences, lexicon-free, so they find metrics the paper never names.Only the blog text"a state-of-the-art supercomputer would require approximately 10,000 years" discerns classical simulation time

A measurement frame is a pattern of words that signals that a sentence reports a measurement. The table lists the frames. Beside each is its lift, which shows how strongly the frame marks a metric sentence in the team's hand-coded set (lift = how much more often the frame appears in sentences coded as metric than elsewhere):

Future tense and hedges work the other way. Words such as "will", "could" and "aims to" mark a sentence as not about a measurement. Some sentences explain a metric by a claim that is not itself about a metric, such as "random circuits have no exploitable structure". Such a sentence produces no node; it is kept as an audit note only. Each metric is one node, mirroring the paper side. Each node lists every mention of its metric, with the quote and the route that found it.

Human raters. People do this extraction. In the Metric Coder, each rater reads a blog and highlights every metric, privately. The highlighted spans are exported with character offsets, so two raters' choices can be compared exactly. Inter-rater reliability, the agreement among the human raters, sets the standard. A language-model pass must match that standard before it is used on the rest of the corpus.

(iv) Interdependencies in a blog

A blog states an interdependency when one sentence ties two metrics with an explicit connective. Such a tie is an edge between the two metrics. "Fidelity falls as depth grows" is an edge from depth to fidelity. Blog edges are coded by a language model. The rule is narrow so that any two readers of the same sentence, human or model, make the same call.

An edge must have three things:

  1. Both ends are metrics already in the blog's list under (iii). A sentence whose other end is not a metric is kept as an audit note.
  2. A connective in the sentence: depends on, scales with, limited by, requires, as X grows Y falls, the more X the less Y, determines, because of. Two metrics mentioned in one sentence with no connective between them are not an edge.
  3. The sentence itself, quoted and stored with the edge, so that the call can be checked.

Each edge points the way the sentence states it, from A to B. Each edge also keeps a type assigned by the language-model coder: faithful, conditions dropped, collapsed, misattributed or invented. The type is a label only; no count uses it. A negated statement, such as "does not depend on", is kept with its polarity and is off by default in every view.

The paper's prose links in (ii) are read with this same rule.

Three sentences show the rule at work. The first two come from IBM's 2019 post on Google's supremacy paper. "an ideal simulation of the same task can be performed on a classical system in 2.5 days and with far greater fidelity" ties nothing: it names two metrics but has no connective. "with additional refinements the classical cost of the simulation can be further reduced" ties nothing either: it names only one metric. Google's own post, by contrast, says "finding the most likely bitstrings for a random quantum circuit on a classical computer becomes exponentially more difficult as the number of qubits (width) and number of gate cycles (depth) grow". That sentence gives two edges, qubit count to classical simulation time and depth to classical simulation time, with the connective "becomes exponentially more difficult as … grow".

Worked example: a short paper on electricity

The paper-side rules can be followed step by step on a small paper about electricity. Imagine a paper with four equations. Its abstract names voltage V, current I, resistance R, power P, charge Q and time t. Its numbered equations are:

(1) V = IR     (2) P = IV     (3) P = I²R     (4) Q = It

Step 1, metrics (i). There are six named quantities: V, I, R, P, Q and t. Each is named in the abstract and appears in a numbered equation.

Step 2, redundancy (ii, rule 2). Add the equations one at a time, and ask whether each one says something new. (1) and (2) do. (3) does not: substitute V = IR into P = IV and the result is P = I²R. Hence (1) and (2) already imply (3), and (3) adds nothing. (4) does say something new. The paper therefore states 3 independent relationships, not 4.

Step 3, minimal tied groups (ii, rule 3). The question is which sets of quantities the three surviving equations force to move together, where no smaller set inside a set already does so. There are eight such sets. Each is listed with the single relation that ties it:

Minimal tied groupRelation the paper entailsSize
{V, I, R}V = IR3
{V, I, P}P = IV3
{I, R, P}P = I²R3
{V, R, P}V² = PR3

{V, R, Q, t}Vt = QR4
{V, P, Q, t}QV = Pt4
{R, P, Q, t}Q²R = Pt²4

The third and fourth groups are the two rearrangements the paper might or might not have written. They exist either way, because (1) and (2) entail them. The last three groups are relationships the paper never wrote down, yet its equations commit it to them.

Step 4, why the unit is dependence and not a shared equation. A simpler rule would count two quantities as tied whenever they share an equation. That count, however, depends on which equations the author chose to write. The table gives every pair of the six quantities under the two readings:

Three things stand out. First, sharing an equation misses six pairs, the bottom block, that the paper's equations tie. It misses them because each of these ties runs through a third quantity. Second, sharing an equation counts I–R and I–P twice, because equation (3) restates them. Third, under dependence every pair is tied, as in any connected system. The information therefore lies in the size of the smallest group, which is 3 for a law the paper wrote and 4 for a tie that runs through a third quantity, and in the rank of 3.

The computation is mechanical. The redundancy test is the Gröbner-basis remainder. A group is tied if eliminating every other quantity from the equations leaves a non-trivial relation among the group alone. A tied group is minimal if no subset of it passes the same test.

Worked example for prose: one blog sentence

Take the sentence from Google's 2019 post quoted above. Four steps run on it, this time on the blog side:

  1. Metrics by frame (iii, discerned). The sentence has two measurement heads, "number of qubits" and "number of gate cycles". They give the metrics qubit count and depth. It also has a scaling frame, "becomes exponentially more difficult as … grow". That frame gives the thing that scales: the difficulty of classical simulation, which is the metric classical simulation time. Nothing in the sentence is a value, so nothing is dropped.
  2. Metrics by alias (iii, recovered). The paper's alias table has n for the number of qubits, m for the number of cycles, and classical cost among the names for its simulation-time quantity. All three blog metrics are therefore recovered as well as discerned. Each node records both routes and the quote.
  3. Edge (iv). One connective ties the metrics: as qubits and depth grow, classical difficulty grows. That gives two edges, qubit count to classical simulation time and depth to classical simulation time. Both point at the same target, and both carry this sentence as their quote.
  4. Preimages. Each recovered blog metric points back at the paper quantities it matched; that set is its preimage, defined fully in the next section. Qubit count maps to {n}, depth to {m}, and classical simulation time to {classical cost}. The two edges therefore imply two paper pairs, n with classical cost and m with classical cost. The next section looks these pairs up in the paper.

The parentheticals "(width)" and "(depth)" are the blog's own glosses on the paper's names, and they are what makes step 2 easy here. A blog that said "bigger circuits are harder to simulate" would discern nothing, because it has no frame. It would recover nothing, because it uses no alias. Hence it would contribute no edge.

Putting both together: checking a blog's ties against the paper

The two sides meet through a mapping. Each blog metric found by route (iii)-recovered points at the paper quantities whose aliases it used. That set of paper quantities is the metric's preimage; read the other way, the blog metric is each such paper quantity's blog image. "53 qubits", for instance, recovers the paper's n, so n is in the preimage of qubit count. A metric found only by the discerned route has an empty preimage. A blog metric whose preimage holds two paper quantities has collapsed them into one word.

A blog edge from A to B, found under (iv), implies every pair of paper quantities that can be formed from pre(A) and pre(B), where pre(A) is the preimage of A. Each implied pair is looked up in the paper's ties: the minimal tied groups from (ii), plus the prose links from (ii) under whichever scopes are switched on. The lookup gives every paper pair one of five statuses:

StatusMeaningReads as
representedthe blog implies it and the paper ties itfaithful transmission
ungroundedthe blog implies it and the paper does not tie itthe blog asserts a dependence the paper never states
unlinkedthe paper ties it, both ends have a blog image, the blog never implies ita dependence the blog had the words for and dropped
orphaned endthe paper ties it and at least one end has no blog imagea dependence the blog could not state because it never names one of the quantities
not linkedneither sidenothing to compare

Two more counts sit beside these statuses. Orphans are paper quantities with no blog image at all. Collapsed pairs are two paper quantities that the blog folds into one metric, each reported with whether the paper ties them. Each blog edge also gets a grounding ratio: the share of the pairs it implies that the paper ties. This ratio sets the width of the edge's arc on the map. Where a tie comes from the equations, the size of its smallest tied group travels with it. Hence a blog edge grounded by a two-quantity law can be told apart from one grounded only through a four-quantity chain.
